# **On-screen helper**

Margaret is 77 and lives alone, with family close enough to visit but too far away to drop in whenever something goes wrong.

She manages her shopping, keeps her appointments on a paper calendar, and understands her monthly income and expenses. Her daughter, Anna, helps with the things that have become awkward: an unfamiliar online form, an account that will not open, an email attachment she needs to download and send back.

Their arrangement has grown gradually. Neither remembers deciding that Anna would become responsible for so much of Margaret’s digital life.

Margaret uses her phone every day. She reads family messages, answers calls, and looks at photographs. Familiar screens are comfortable. She can follow the steps she knows. But when an unfamiliar screen appears, she becomes unsure about what to do. She reads the instructions again, looking for something that matches but hesitates: *‘I don’t know what to do next,*’ and *‘could trying again make things worse’*? Often, she leaves it until Anna can help.

She is willing to accept help. What bothers her is having to organize ordinary parts of her life around when help is available.

## **How paying a bill became something they do together**

For years, Margaret paid her household bills on paper. She checked the amount, wrote the check, and recorded the payment. She could see what she had done.

More recently, Anna helped her start paying the electricity company online. They set up her account together. Anna saved the website and wrote the steps on a sheet of paper: where to sign in, where to find the bill, which button to press.

For a while, it worked. Margaret kept the instructions beside her phone and followed them each month.

Then the website changed.

The button in Anna’s instructions was no longer there. A sign-in screen asked for a code sent by email. Margaret knew how to open her email, but returning to the payment page was another matter. She found herself opening the website again and wondering whether she had started a second attempt.

She called Anna, who was at work.

Anna saw her mother’s name and stepped into the hallway. She had ten minutes before her next meeting.

“Everything okay?”

“Yes, I’m just trying to do the electricity. It’s asking me for something that isn’t on your instructions.”

“Okay. What does it say?”

Margaret began reading from the top of the screen. Anna listened, trying to picture the page.

“Can you see ‘Payments’?”

“No. It says ‘Verify.’”

“What does it say underneath that?”

Margaret brings the phone closer.

“‘We’ve sent a code to…’ Yes, that’s my email address.”

“Okay. You’ll need that code to finish signing in. Can you open your email?”

“Do I close this?”

“Leave that page open. Just switch to your email.”

“I’m not sure how to get back there.”

“I’ll talk you through it. First, go to your home screen, like you do when you want to open your messages.”

Margaret hesitated, then returned to the home screen.

“Okay. I can see my apps.”

“Open your email.”

Margaret put the call on speaker and searched for the email. She found a message from the electricity company, but she could not tell whether it was the new one or the one from her earlier attempt.

“There are two of them.”

“Did you try signing in earlier?”

“Yes, before I called you.”

“All right. Can you see the time beside each message?”

Margaret opened one, then returned to the inbox.

“I can’t tell which one you mean.”

Anna glanced through the meeting-room window. Her colleagues were gathering.

“Okay, Mum. I’ll look here.”

Margaret had previously given Anna access to her email so she could help with account problems. Anna opened her mother’s inbox on her own phone and found the newest verification message.

“I’ve found it. Can you get back to the electricity page?”

Margaret switched screens.

“It’s asking me to sign in again.”

Anna paused. Finding the email had been straightforward. Getting her mother back to the right place was still difficult when she could not see the screen.

“Don’t request another code yet. Tell me exactly what it says.”

“Are you busy?”

“I’ve got a meeting starting.”

“I’m sorry. I thought it would be quick.”

“It’s okay, Mum. Let’s leave it for now. I’ll look with you tonight.”

After they hung up, Anna wrote “Mum—electricity” at the edge of her meeting notes. She could access the accounts, but she still needed time to deal with them.

That evening, Anna called with her laptop open. Margaret had previously given her access to the electricity account as well as her email, so she could help when things went wrong.

“Let me bring it up here, Mum.”

Anna signed in, retrieved the verification code from Margaret’s inbox, and checked the payment history. No payment had been made for the current bill.

“It’s still unpaid. Do you want me to do it now?”

“Yes, please. I don’t want to go through all that again.”

Anna read out the bill amount and confirmed which saved bank account Margaret wanted to use. Margaret agreed, and Anna submitted the payment.

“That’s done. I’ve got the confirmation.”

“Oh, good. Thank you.”

Margaret put away her instructions. Anna closed the laptop and went back to her evening.

The bill was paid, and both felt relieved. But they had finished by moving the task onto Anna’s screen. Margaret had never found her way through the steps on her own phone.

The next month, when the electricity email arrived, she called Anna before trying.

## **Meeting Eliza**

Margaret receives her Eliza tablet and places it on the kitchen table.

When she turns it on, the screen opens to a simple greeting. There are no rows of unfamiliar apps to choose from.

“Hi, I’m Eliza. What’s your name?”

The words appear in large text as Eliza speaks. Eliza's words fill in on screen as it speaks, and a Stop button lets her cut it short. When Eliza asks her something, the screen shows "Listening…" so she knows she can answer aloud. There is always a Type button beside it.

“Margaret.”

"Hello, Margaret. Which language would you like us to use? You can say it, or tap one."

English, Español and Français appear on screen, with More languages.

"English."

They begin by making the tablet comfortable for her.

“Now, how does this speaking pace feel? Would you like me to speak slower, faster, or stay at this speed?”

“A little slower.”

Eliza adjusts its pace.

“How does this sound?”

“Yes, that’s better.”

“I’ll use this pace. You can ask me to change it anytime.”

Next, Eliza asks about the words on the screen.

“Can you read this comfortably, or would you like it larger?”

“A bit larger.”

The text grows while Margaret watches.

“Another little bit.”

It grows again. Margaret moves the tablet back to where she would naturally leave it on the table.

“That’s good.”

“Your choices are saved. You can ask me to change them whenever you like.”

Margaret has already learned something about using Eliza: she can say what she needs, see what changes, and correct it. She does not have to find a settings menu or get everything right on the first try.

## **What Eliza does**

Once Margaret is comfortable, Eliza introduces its purpose in one sentence.

“I help you get everyday things done on this tablet by understanding what’s on your screen, guiding you through confusing steps, and doing the parts you ask me to—with you in control.”

Then it shows her where to find that help.

“When you wake your tablet, you’ll see this.”

The screen shows a large **Talk to Eliza** button and a clearly labeled **Close** button.

“Tap ‘Talk to Eliza’ when you need me. Choose ‘Close’ when you want to use the tablet on your own.”

Margaret tries closing it.

The greeting disappears, leaving a small Eliza button at the edge of the screen.

“I’ll also be here while you’re using the tablet. Tap this button whenever you want help.”

The button briefly highlights. Margaret taps it, and Eliza opens again.

She knows how to put Eliza away and bring it back.

## **Starting with something that matters**

“What task do you need help with today?”

Margaret looks at the sheet of instructions she keeps beside her phone.

“I need to pay my electricity bill. My daughter usually helps me.”

“We can work through that together. How do you receive the bill—by email or on paper?”

“Email. It’s on my phone.”

“You can open that same email on this tablet. Which email service do you use?”

“Gmail, I think.”

Eliza shows the Gmail name and symbol.

“Does this look familiar?”

“Yes, that’s it.”

“Would you like to connect your Gmail account so I can find your electricity bill for you? It also lets me help find sign-in codes when you need them.”

“Yes, please.”

Eliza asks first, then opens Google's own sign-in page and moves to the side of the screen. A label beside each box tells her what to type. "This is Google's own page. Only Google sees your password." Margaret enters her Gmail address and password, then completes Google's verification.

Once sign-in succeeds, the tablet’s password manager offers to save those details.

“Would you like to save your Gmail username and password? Your password manager can help enter them when you need to sign in again.”

“Yes, please.”

Margaret confirms the save.

“They’re saved. If someone you trust helps with your accounts, you can share your usernames and passwords with them. You can share all of them or choose specific accounts.”

“My daughter Anna helps me with that.”

“Would you like to set up her access now, or finish your electricity bill first?”

“The bill first.”

“Of course. Nothing has been shared. Let’s finish connecting Gmail so I can find the bill.”

Google displays the connection permissions. Eliza points out that Google is also asking to manage her contacts, which the bill doesn't need. "You can untick it on Google's page, and I'll still work." Margaret decides; Eliza never unticks it herself. Eliza explains what access is being requested, including whether it extends beyond the messages needed for this bill.

Margaret reviews and approves the connection.

The Google page closes, returning her to Eliza.

“Gmail is now connected. I see an email from Edison that says your bill is ready. Is Edison your electricity company?”

“Yes.”

“Would you like me to open it?”

“Please.”

Eliza opens the attached bill and presents the company name, service address, amount, and due date in Margaret’s preferred text size. A “View original bill” button sits beneath the details.

“I’ve made the key details easier to read. You can open the original bill below, or ask me to show you any part of it.”

“Take your time. Let me know when you’re ready to continue, or ask me about anything on the bill.”

Margaret reads it.

“All right. I’d like to pay it today.”

“Would you like me to open Edison’s website and take you to the sign-in page?”

“Yes, please.”

## **Paying the bill with Eliza**

A blue cursor with a small ‘Eliza’ label appears on the screen, showing Margaret where Eliza is about to tap.

“This blue pointer shows where I’m going to tap. You can say ‘Wait’ or tap ‘Pause’ at any time.”

Margaret watches the blue Eliza cursor move to the browser icon. When it selects the icon, a small ring pulses outward from the cursor, making the tap visible. When the browser opens, the cursor moves to the address bar. A brief pulse marks the tap, then a blue outline stays around the bar to show it is selected. A blinking text caret appears inside, and Margaret watches Edison’s website address appear as Eliza types. 

Eliza enters Edison’s verified website address and loads the page. A short caption reads **“Opening Edison.”**

When the homepage finishes loading, Eliza says, “I’ve kept your bill details handy. Let’s sign in to your Edison account.”

The pointer moves to the sign-in link. A blue outline appears around it, and the pointer pauses briefly before selecting it. Margaret can follow which control Eliza is using and what happens next.

The sign-in page opens. The pointer stops moving, and the email-address field is highlighted.

Eliza identifies what the sign-in field requires—an email address or a username—and gives a matching instruction. In Margaret’s case, the field asks for an email address.

Eliza highlights the email field and the keyboard opens. Beside the field, Eliza asks: "Use margaret.h@gmail.com?" It's the address from the Gmail account she connected earlier. Margaret says "Yes," and Eliza enters it.

Next, Eliza highlights the password field.

"Type your Edison password." A short label beside the password field repeats it.

Margaret checks her notebook and types the password. Concealed dots appear in the field, with a clearly labeled Show password button beside it. She can correct any character with the keyboard.

(Once she's saved the password in the password manager, as she did for Gmail, this step becomes another offer at the field.)

When the password is entered, Eliza highlights “Sign in.”

Margaret taps “Sign in.” The password is concealed again as the sign-in proceeds.

Then the company asks for an email verification code.

Margaret recognizes the screen.

“This is where I get stuck.”

“The company has just sent a code to your connected email. I can bring it here.”

“Please.”

Eliza retrieves the message matching this sign-in attempt. A field offer identifies the company and when the email arrived, with Yes / No, I'll type. Margaret taps Yes, Eliza enters the code, and she continues.

She has not left the page. There is nothing to remember and no route to retrace.

The account opens.

## **Checking before paying**

After the verification code is accepted, Edison’s account overview opens. Eliza has retained the electricity bill details – billing period, service address, account id, amount, and due date – into context.

“You’re signed in. This is your Edison account overview. Let’s check that it matches your bill.”

Eliza brings the service address into view and highlights it. Its spoken question also appears as a short caption.

“Is this your address?”

“Yes, that’s mine.”

“That matches the bill you showed me.”

Still on the account overview, Eliza scrolls to the current balance. It highlights the amount due and due date.

“This amount also matches your bill. Take a moment to check it.”

Margaret reads the numbers.

“Yes, that looks right.”

“Before we pay, shall I check whether a payment is already scheduled? That way it won't be paid twice.”

“Yes, please. Anna sometimes does it for me.”

“Then let’s look at the payment activity.”

Eliza selects the website’s payment-activity link. A new page opens. It brings pending and scheduled payments into view.

“Edison isn’t showing a pending or scheduled payment here. Has Anna told you she’s paid this one?”

“No. I told her I wanted to try it myself.”

“All right. Shall I open the payment form?”

“Yes, please.”

Eliza selects “Make a payment.” The form opens, showing the available payment methods.

A checking account is already saved with Edison. Eliza highlights its label and last four digits.

“There’s a checking account saved here, ending in 2048\. Is that the one you want to use?”

Margaret checks her notebook.

“Yes, that’s the one.”

Eliza selects it. The website marks that account as the payment method.

“Selected. Shall I fill in the full amount and today's date? Nothing is paid yet.”

“Yes, please.”

The amount field fills with the bill total. Eliza selects today’s date, then brings both fields into view so Margaret can inspect them.

“The full amount is entered, with today as the payment date.”

Margaret notices an option beneath them.

“What’s that about automatic payments?”

Eliza pauses and highlights the option without selecting it.

“That would let Edison collect future payments automatically. It’s currently off.”

“Leave it off. I want to check each bill.”

“I’ll leave it off. We’re preparing just this payment.”

Eliza brings the website’s “Review payment” button into view.

“Shall I open the review page? That won't send the payment.”

“Yes.”

It selects the button. Edison’s review page opens, showing the amount, payment date, and selected checking account.

“Here are the details together. Nothing has been paid yet. Take your time checking them, and tell me if anything needs to change.”

## **Knowing it is finished**

When she is ready, Margaret taps the final payment button herself.

The website begins processing.

“Your payment has been submitted. We’re waiting for the company’s response,” Eliza says. The same message remains on the screen. “There’s no need to press again.”

Margaret waits.

When the company returns a confirmation, Eliza shows it alongside a plain-language result: the company has confirmed receipt of her payment for this bill. The amount, date, and confirmation number are visible.

“Edison has confirmed your payment, and I’ve saved the receipt. Take your time looking it over. When you’re ready, say ‘I’m done’ or tap **Done**.”

Margaret checks the amount and date.

“I’m done.”

“Before we finish, would you like Email and Electricity buttons on your home screen so you can get back to those websites easily?”

Two large tiles appear: **Email — Gmail** and **Electricity — Edison**, with options to save either, both, or **Not now**.

“Save both.”

Eliza adds the shortcuts and shows Margaret where they are.

“They’re saved. Email opens Gmail, and Electricity opens Edison. You can still tap my button whenever you need help.”

“Thank you.”

Margaret closes Eliza and sets the tablet down.

That afternoon, she has a moment of doubt. Previously, this would have meant calling Anna or opening the account and risking another confusing sequence.

Instead, she wakes the tablet. The **Talk to Eliza** button is waiting where she learned to find it.

She taps it.

“Show me what we did with the electricity.”

The receipt comes back.

She can check without needing someone else to check for her.

## **A different kind of visit**

When Anna comes at the weekend, Margaret mentions that she paid the bill.

“Did it all work?”

“Yes. I can show you.”

Margaret brings up the confirmation on the tablet. Anna can see the company, the amount, and the result without retracing the entire task. Margaret has something more useful than “I think I did it.”

Anna puts the tablet back on its stand.

For this bill, there is nothing left for either of them to remember, chase, or repair.

Margaret reaches for the kettle. They have the rest of the visit.
